## Week 4

### Paper 1 — Grelotti et al. (2005)
**"fMRI activation of the fusiform gyrus and amygdala to cartoon characters but not to faces in a boy with autism"**

Alright, so this week we're looking at neural mechanisms in autism, and our first paper is a really neat case study from Grelotti and colleagues, published in 2005.

Here's the setup. There's a region in your brain called the fusiform gyrus — it sits on the underside of the temporal lobe — and in most people, it lights up reliably when you look at faces. It's so consistent that it's often called the "fusiform face area." The assumption for a long time was that this region is essentially hardwired for faces — that it's a dedicated face processor.

Now, what Grelotti's team did was scan a boy with autism using fMRI. This boy had the typical pattern you see in autism research — reduced fusiform activation to human faces. But here's where it gets interesting. He had an intense interest in Digimon — the cartoon characters — and when they showed him Digimon characters in the scanner, his fusiform gyrus and his amygdala lit up strongly. More than they did for faces.

So what does this tell us? It challenges the idea that the fusiform gyrus is just a face module. Instead, it suggests that what really drives this region is expertise and personal salience — things you've spent a lot of time learning to distinguish between, things that matter to you. For most of us, that's faces, because we're socially motivated from birth. But for this boy, that motivation was directed elsewhere, and the neural machinery followed.

It's a single case, so we need to be cautious, but it raises some really powerful questions. If the fusiform gyrus follows interest and expertise rather than being hardwired for faces, then is the face processing deficit in autism really about faces per se, or is it about what the individual finds rewarding and worth attending to? Does reduced social motivation early in development mean the brain never gets the chance to specialise for faces in the first place? Could you, in principle, redirect that specialisation — if you made faces more rewarding, would the fusiform follow? And more broadly, how much of what we call "innate" brain organisation is actually shaped by where we direct our attention throughout development? These are the kinds of questions this paper opens up, and they're worth holding in mind as you read through it.

### Paper 2 — Keown et al. (2013)
**"Local functional overconnectivity in posterior brain regions is associated with symptom severity in autism spectrum disorders"**

So our second paper this week takes a step back from individual brain regions and looks at how different parts of the brain talk to each other in autism. This is Keown and colleagues, published in Cell Reports in 2013.

For a while, the dominant theory in autism neuroscience was the "underconnectivity hypothesis" — the idea that long-range connections between distant brain regions are weaker in autism, which might explain difficulties with complex tasks that require integrating information across the brain. And there's decent evidence for that. But Keown's team asked a more nuanced question: what about local connections? What's happening within individual brain regions?

What they found, using resting-state fMRI, was that posterior brain regions — particularly visual and sensory areas — showed local overconnectivity in individuals with autism. Nearby neurons were talking to each other more than you'd expect, not less. And critically, the degree of this local overconnectivity correlated with symptom severity — more overconnectivity, more severe symptoms.

This gives us a really interesting picture. It's not simply that the autistic brain is underconnected. Instead, there seems to be a trade-off: regions are overly connected locally, perhaps at the cost of efficient long-range integration. You can think of it like a city where every neighbourhood has incredibly dense internal roads, but the motorways connecting neighbourhoods are underdeveloped. Information gets processed intensely within local circuits, but integrating across the whole brain becomes harder.

As you read this paper, think about what this pattern might mean for the sensory experiences often reported in autism — the heightened sensitivity, the intense focus on detail. Could local overconnectivity be the neural basis of that? And how does this reshape your understanding of what "connectivity" means in the brain — is more always better, or is it about the right balance?

## Week 5

### Paper 1 — Mancini et al. (2011)
**"Whole-body mapping of spatial acuity for pain and touch"**

This week we're moving into the somatosensory system — how the brain processes touch and pain across the body. Our first paper is from Mancini and colleagues, published in 2011.

Here's something you might take for granted: if someone pokes you on the fingertip, you can tell exactly where they touched you. But if someone pokes you in the middle of your back, your spatial precision drops off dramatically. This variation in spatial acuity across the body surface is well established for touch — it roughly follows the density of sensory receptors in the skin and the amount of cortical territory devoted to each body part. That's your classic somatosensory homunculus.

But what about pain? Is the spatial acuity of pain distributed the same way as touch, or does it follow its own map? That's what Mancini's team set out to test. They systematically measured spatial acuity for both touch and pain across the entire body surface.

What they found was that pain and touch share broadly similar spatial patterns — areas with high tactile acuity, like the hands and face, also tend to have better pain localisation. But the maps aren't identical. Pain acuity is generally coarser than touch, and some body regions showed dissociations between the two. This suggests that while pain and touch share some common neural architecture — likely at the level of primary somatosensory cortex — they also rely on partially distinct processing pathways.

Think about what this means for clinical practice. If a patient reports diffuse pain, is that because the injury itself is diffuse, or because that body region simply has poor pain localisation? And what does this tell us about how the brain constructs a spatial model of the body — is there one unified body map, or multiple overlapping maps for different modalities?

### Paper 2 — Goldstein et al. (2018)
**"Brain-to-brain coupling during handholding is associated with pain reduction"**

Our second paper this week is from Goldstein and colleagues, 2018, and it's a fascinating study that sits right at the intersection of social neuroscience and pain research.

The basic finding is this: when romantic partners hold hands while one of them is in pain, their brainwaves synchronise — and the more they synchronise, the more the pain is reduced. That's a remarkable claim, so let's unpack how they tested it.

They used EEG to measure brain activity simultaneously in both partners — this is called hyperscanning — across several conditions: sitting together, sitting apart, holding hands, and not holding hands, with and without pain. What they found was that inter-brain coupling — the correlation between the two people's neural signals — increased during handholding, and increased further when one partner was in pain. Most importantly, the degree of this coupling in the alpha frequency band predicted how much pain relief the partner in pain actually experienced.

The mechanism they propose involves the empathic accuracy of the non-suffering partner. The more empathic the partner, the stronger the brain-to-brain coupling, and the greater the analgesic effect. It's as if the non-suffering partner's brain is modelling the other's pain state, and this neural alignment somehow facilitates pain regulation.

This raises some genuinely profound questions. How does one person's brain activity physically influence another person's pain experience? What's the pathway — is it through touch, through autonomic synchronisation, through some top-down modulation of pain circuits? And what does this mean for our understanding of pain as a purely individual experience? This paper challenges us to think about pain as something that exists between people, not just within them.

## Week 7

### Paper 1 — Victor et al. (2007)
**"A common polymorphism in the brain-derived neurotrophic factor gene (BDNF) modulates human cortical plasticity and the response to rTMS"**

This week we're getting into brain plasticity and neuromodulation. Our first paper is from Victor and colleagues, 2007, and it connects genetics to how plastic your brain is.

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor — BDNF — is one of the most important molecules for synaptic plasticity. It's involved in long-term potentiation, synapse strengthening, and learning. There's a well-known genetic variant in the BDNF gene called the Val66Met polymorphism — some people carry a valine at position 66, others carry a methionine. About a third of the population carries at least one Met allele. Previous work had suggested that the Met variant is associated with reduced activity-dependent secretion of BDNF, which could mean less efficient plasticity.

What Victor's team did was use repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation — rTMS — to probe cortical plasticity directly in humans with different BDNF genotypes. They applied rTMS protocols known to induce short-term plasticity in motor cortex and measured the effects using motor evoked potentials.

The key finding was that individuals with the Val/Val genotype showed robust plasticity responses to rTMS, while Met carriers showed significantly reduced responses. In other words, a single genetic variation predicted how much the brain's excitability could be shifted by external stimulation.

This has wide-reaching implications. If your BDNF genotype affects how plastic your cortex is, it could influence everything from how you respond to rehabilitation after a stroke, to how effective brain stimulation therapies are for depression. Should we be genotyping patients before prescribing rTMS? Does this variant affect learning and memory in everyday life? And more fundamentally, how much of the variation we see in people's ability to learn and adapt is baked into their genes versus shaped by experience?

### Paper 2 — Rao et al. (2018)
**"Neurofeedback: an alternative treatment approach for autism spectrum disorder"**

Our second paper this week shifts from plasticity to application. This is Rao and colleagues, 2018, and it's about neurofeedback as a potential intervention for autism.

Neurofeedback is a form of biofeedback where people learn to regulate their own brain activity in real time. You record someone's EEG, extract a feature of interest — say, the ratio between theta and beta power — and feed it back to them as a visual or auditory signal. Over repeated sessions, the person learns to shift their brain activity in the desired direction. It's essentially operant conditioning for brainwaves.

Rao and colleagues reviewed the evidence for using neurofeedback to address some of the core and associated features of autism — things like attention difficulties, social communication challenges, and executive function deficits. The rationale is straightforward: if autism is associated with atypical patterns of neural activity — which the EEG literature suggests it is — then perhaps training people to normalise those patterns could improve symptoms.

The evidence they review is cautiously promising. Several studies report improvements in attention, behaviour, and social functioning following neurofeedback training, and some of these gains persisted at follow-up. But the field has significant methodological challenges — small sample sizes, lack of proper sham controls, and the question of whether any improvements are really due to the neurofeedback itself or to the attention and therapeutic relationship involved in sitting with a clinician for twenty sessions.

As you read this, think critically about the assumptions underlying the approach. Is "normalising" atypical brain activity a valid therapeutic goal? Who decides what the target pattern should be? And how do we disentangle the specific effects of neurofeedback from the non-specific effects of simply spending structured time with a supportive adult? These are important questions not just for neurofeedback, but for any intervention approach in neurodevelopmental conditions.

## Week 8

### Paper 1 — Bishop et al. (2004)
**"Prefrontal cortical function and anxiety: controlling attention to threat-related stimuli"**

This week we're looking at anxiety, fear, and the amygdala. Our first paper is from Bishop and colleagues, 2004, published in Nature Neuroscience.

Here's the basic question: when you're anxious, you tend to get distracted by threatening things in your environment — angry faces, potential dangers, ambiguous stimuli that might be threatening. This attentional bias towards threat is one of the most reliable findings in anxiety research. But what's the neural mechanism? Is it that the amygdala is overreacting to threat, or is it that the prefrontal cortex is failing to control attention?

Bishop's team used fMRI to test this. They had people with varying levels of trait anxiety perform an attention task where threatening faces appeared as distractors. The clever bit was manipulating the expectedness of these distractors — sometimes threatening faces appeared frequently, sometimes rarely.

What they found was that the prefrontal cortex — specifically the rostral anterior cingulate and lateral prefrontal regions — was responsible for controlling attention to threat-related distractors. When threat distractors were expected, the prefrontal cortex ramped up to suppress them. But here's the critical finding: in people with higher trait anxiety, this prefrontal recruitment was significantly reduced, particularly when threats were frequent and predictable.

So the problem in anxiety isn't necessarily that the threat detection system is overactive — it's that the cognitive control system fails to suppress it. The prefrontal cortex doesn't step in to say "I know that's there, but it's not relevant right now." This bridges two big literatures — cognitive control and clinical anxiety — by showing they share neural substrates.

Think about what this means therapeutically. If anxiety is partly a failure of prefrontal control, does that explain why cognitive behavioural therapy works — by training prefrontal engagement? And is there a tipping point where the system becomes overwhelmed and anxiety becomes self-reinforcing?

### Paper 2 — Feinstein et al. (2011)
**"The human amygdala and the induction and experience of fear"**

Our second paper this week is one of the most striking case studies in affective neuroscience. This is Feinstein and colleagues, 2011, and it centres on a patient known as SM.

SM has a rare genetic condition called Urbach-Wiethe disease, which caused selective bilateral calcification of her amygdalae — essentially, both her amygdalae were destroyed, with the rest of her brain left largely intact. This makes her extraordinarily valuable for understanding what the amygdala actually does, because you can see what's missing when it's gone.

What Feinstein's team did was expose SM to a range of fear-inducing stimuli — and not just in the lab. They took her to an exotic pet store and let her handle snakes and spiders, which she'd previously said she hated and avoided. They took her through a haunted house. They showed her horror film clips. In every case, SM showed no fear. She approached the snakes with curiosity, she laughed through the haunted house, she found the horror films interesting rather than frightening. She reported no fear, showed no avoidance, and displayed no physiological fear responses.

But here's what makes this more than just a "patient can't feel fear" story. SM could experience other emotions — happiness, sadness, disgust — perfectly normally. Her general cognition was intact. She could recognise fear in others and understand the concept of fear intellectually. She just couldn't feel it herself or generate the behavioural programme that goes with it.

This tells us something very specific about the amygdala: it's not a general-purpose emotion centre. It has a particular and pivotal role in fear — specifically in triggering the whole cascade of experience, physiology, and behaviour that constitutes a fear response. Without that trigger, the experience simply doesn't happen. Consider what this means for the relationship between knowing something is dangerous and actually feeling afraid. Can you have one without the other? SM suggests you can — and that has profound implications for how we think about risk, decision-making, and what fear is actually for.

## Week 9

### Paper 1 — de Gelder et al. (2008)
**"Intact navigation skills after bilateral loss of striate cortex"**

This week we're exploring blindsight — one of the most counterintuitive phenomena in neuroscience. Our first paper is from de Gelder and colleagues, 2008.

Patient TN is clinically blind. He suffered two successive strokes that destroyed his primary visual cortex — area V1 — in both hemispheres. He has no conscious visual experience. Standard clinical tests confirm he cannot see. He uses a cane to get around. And yet — and this is the remarkable part — when researchers placed him in a corridor filled with obstacles and asked him to walk through it without his cane, he navigated around every single object. He didn't bump into anything. He turned his body to squeeze past obstacles. And when asked afterwards, he had no awareness that the obstacles were there. He thought the corridor was empty.

This is blindsight taken to an extraordinary level. Previous demonstrations of blindsight typically involved simple forced-choice tasks in the lab — detecting whether a stimulus appeared on the left or right. TN's case showed that visually guided behaviour without awareness can extend to complex, whole-body navigation in a naturalistic environment.

The explanation lies in the visual pathways that bypass V1. There are several subcortical routes — through the superior colliculus and pulvinar, for instance — that can carry visual information to motor and spatial processing areas without it ever reaching conscious awareness. In sighted people, these pathways are largely overshadowed by the dominant V1 route. But when V1 is gone, they can sustain surprisingly sophisticated spatial behaviour.

What does this mean for our understanding of consciousness and vision? We tend to equate seeing with visual awareness, but TN shows they're dissociable. How much of your daily visual behaviour is actually conscious? When you dodge someone on a busy pavement, are you consciously processing their position, or is a lot of that handled by these faster, unconscious pathways? And what does it mean to "see" something if you can act on it without knowing it's there?

### Paper 2 — Tamietto et al. (2009)
**"Unseen facial and bodily expressions trigger fast emotional reactions"**

Our second paper this week builds on blindsight but takes it into emotional territory. This is Tamietto and colleagues, 2009, published in PNAS.

We've just seen with TN that you can navigate without conscious vision. But can you also respond emotionally to things you can't see? That's what Tamietto's team tested. They presented emotional expressions — both faces and bodies — to the blind visual field of patients with cortical damage, and measured automatic facial reactions using electromyography, or EMG. EMG picks up tiny muscle movements in the face — the kind of micro-expressions you produce unconsciously when you see someone smile or grimace.

The findings were striking. Even though the patients couldn't consciously see the emotional stimuli, their faces automatically mirrored the emotion expressed. Happy expressions triggered activity in the smile muscles, fearful expressions triggered activity in the frown muscles. And here's the really interesting bit: the reactions to unseen stimuli were actually faster than reactions to stimuli the patients could consciously see, and they produced higher physiological arousal.

But the authors argue this isn't simple mimicry — it's something deeper. They showed that visually very different stimuli — a fearful face versus a fearful body posture — produced remarkably similar facial responses. If this were mimicry, you'd expect the face to copy the specific motor pattern it saw. Instead, the patients were responding to the emotional meaning of the expression, regardless of its visual form. The authors call this affective resonance rather than motor mimicry.

This raises fascinating questions about the nature of emotional processing. If you can resonate with someone's emotion without even being aware of seeing them, how much of our social emotional life is operating below the surface of consciousness? Is empathy fundamentally a conscious process, or is it built on these rapid, automatic mechanisms? And what happens when these unconscious emotional pathways are intact but conscious emotional processing is impaired — could this explain some of the dissociations we see in clinical conditions?

## Week 10

### Paper 1 — Bolognini et al. (2013)
**"Induction of mirror-touch synaesthesia by increasing somatosensory cortical excitability"**

For our final week, we're looking at synaesthesia and face recognition. Our first paper is from Bolognini and colleagues, 2013.

Mirror-touch synaesthesia is a fascinating condition where seeing someone else being touched causes you to feel the touch on your own body. If a mirror-touch synaesthete watches you being tapped on your left cheek, they feel a tap on their own cheek. It's estimated to affect about 1-2% of the population, and it's thought to relate to an overactive mirror system — the neural machinery that maps observed actions and sensations onto your own body representations.

What Bolognini's team asked was: is mirror-touch synaesthesia a qualitatively different brain, or is it an extreme version of something everyone has? To test this, they used transcranial direct current stimulation — tDCS — to increase the excitability of somatosensory cortex and premotor cortex in non-synaesthetes. Then they tested whether these people started showing synaesthesia-like effects.

And they did. After excitatory stimulation, non-synaesthetes showed crossmodal interference effects that are normally only seen in mirror-touch synaesthetes — they were slower to report where they'd been touched when they simultaneously watched someone else being touched in a different location. Crucially, the strength of this effect was modulated by the individual's empathic capacity — more empathic people showed stronger effects.

This is a powerful finding. It suggests that all of us have the neural architecture for mirror-touch synaesthesia — the mechanisms are universal — but in most people, they operate below the threshold of conscious awareness. Synaesthetes don't have a fundamentally different brain; they have a more excitable version of the same system. Think about what this means for the boundary between "normal" and "atypical" perception. Is synaesthesia really a discrete condition, or is it one end of a continuum? And what role does somatosensory excitability play in empathy more broadly — if you literally feel what others feel, does that make you more empathic, or is it the other way around?

### Paper 2 — Bentin et al. (1999)
**"Selective visual streaming in face recognition: evidence from developmental prosopagnosia"**

Our final paper is from Bentin and colleagues, 1999, and it's about a patient with developmental prosopagnosia — a lifelong inability to recognise faces.

There's an ERP component called the N170 — it's a negative voltage deflection that peaks about 170 milliseconds after you see a stimulus, and in most people it's significantly larger for faces than for other objects. It's thought to reflect the early categorisation stage where the visual system says "this is a face" and routes it into specialised face-processing machinery. Think of it as a sorting mechanism — a gate that streams face information into the dedicated face network.

Bentin's patient was a young man who had never been able to recognise faces. He could describe faces in detail — he could tell you someone had blue eyes, a large nose, a round chin — but he couldn't put these features together into a recognisable identity. He'd fail to recognise family members, colleagues, even himself in photographs. And yet his general visual abilities were intact — he could recognise objects, read, navigate, and his visual memory was normal.

When they recorded his ERPs, his N170 showed no face specificity whatsoever. It responded equally to faces and objects. The sorting gate was broken — his visual system wasn't flagging faces as special at the earliest processing stage.

This tells us something fundamental about face recognition. It's not enough to have good general vision and good memory. Face recognition requires a specific early streaming process — a mechanism that identifies a stimulus as a face within the first 170 milliseconds and routes it into dedicated processing channels. Without this streaming step, all the downstream face-processing machinery is starved of input. The patient could see faces perfectly well as visual objects, but because they were never categorised as faces early on, the specialised recognition system never got engaged.

What does this tell us about how the brain organises expertise more generally? Is this streaming mechanism unique to faces, or does it exist for other categories we become expert with — like words for a literate person, or cars for a car enthusiast? And does this connect back to what we discussed in week four with the Grelotti case — is the fusiform face area downstream of exactly this kind of early categorisation process?
